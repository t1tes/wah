Letter 6: 206

Dear JM, I still think about the fact that your first heartbreak was never really because of a guy; it was because of someone you thought would always come back. And I think that says a lot about you. Not because it means you were weak or held on too long, but because even when you were young, you had this belief that people you loved were supposed to stay. I think that is such a difficult thing to lose, the realization that sometimes people can love you and still not know how to show up for you, and that sometimes the people who are supposed to protect you can still leave wounds behind. I wonder if there were moments where you felt like you didn't belong in your own home, like you were constantly trying to figure out what you were doing wrong, or like you were carrying the feeling that maybe you were too much, or maybe you were never enough. And I hate that you ever had to feel that way. There is something so painful about learning when you're young that you are your own person, that the world does not automatically feel what you feel, and that when you fall, when you get hurt, or when something breaks inside you, you are the only one who can truly feel that pain. It's such a lonely thing to realize. Lately, you've been realizing how impossible it is to fully understand your own life from the outside. There are probably people who have looked at your choices and thought they were irresponsible, irrational, or completely against what they believe is the right path. But inside your own mind, those choices made sense. Your ambitions are different. The mission you've given yourself is different. The future you're trying to build is different. And then, as you get older, you learn something even harder: that even the people you love most can never completely enter your mind and experience the world exactly the way you do. They can try, they can listen, and they can hold your hand, but there will always be parts of you that only you know. And I think that's something you've carried for a long time. You've always been someone who feels like you have to explain yourself, like you have to prove why something hurt, or like you have to make people understand before you're allowed to feel it. But you don't. You don't have to make every scar understandable for it to have mattered. I remember how you talked about shame: not just being embarrassed about something you did, but something deeper, almost like being embarrassed by your own existence, feeling too visible, or feeling like even the things your body does naturally can somehow feel like something you have to apologize for. And I think that's such a heavy thing to carry: to feel like you are a burden before you even say anything, or to feel like people are only seeing the mess and not the person underneath. But Jayme, I hope you know that when I look at you, I don't see someone who needs to apologize for existing. I see the person who sings when she's happy, the person who calls her family, and the person who finds small reasons to keep going even on days where everything feels heavier than it should. I think sometimes you forget how much you already do. You think surviving something means you didn't really live through it, but you did; you were there, you experienced it, and you became someone because of it. And maybe that's why you're so afraid of being loved sometimes: because when someone sees the worst parts of you, there's this fear that they'll only see those parts, that they'll decide that's all you are. But people are never only their worst moments, and you aren't either. I think about how shame can create this entire world inside someone's head, a place where every mistake gets replayed, where every insecurity becomes proof, and where you convince yourself that if someone really knew everything, they would leave. But that place only exists inside your mind. You carry it so vividly that sometimes it feels more real than everything around you. And I wish you could see yourself the way other people see you sometimes: not as someone who has to earn kindness, or as someone who has to constantly prove they deserve to be here, but just as Jayme. The same Jayme who makes weird jokes, who loves random things, who gets excited over little details, and who somehow manages to make people care about the things she cares about. I think children who grow up feeling like love can disappear become adults who are scared of finally having it. They want someone to stay, but they're always waiting for the moment they won't; they want to be chosen, but they're already preparing themselves to be left behind. And maybe that's why loving someone can feel so terrifying, because what happens when someone stays? What happens when someone sees all the things you thought would make them leave and they're still there? I think that kind of love can almost feel unfamiliar. But Jayme, I hope you know that you don't have to become someone else to deserve being loved. You don't have to erase every difficult part of your story, and you don't have to pretend nothing happened. Your past is part of you, but it is not the only thing you are. You are also the person who laughs, the person who dreams, the person who gets excited, and the person who looks at the world and still finds things worth caring about. And maybe that's the thing I hope you remember most: you have lived through moments where you thought you wouldn't, you have stood in places where you thought you would never leave, and somehow, you're here, still becoming, still learning, still you.

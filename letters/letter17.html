One last time

Dear Jayme, We ate at Yellow River, got a Nutella waffle, and I remember being so embarrassed. I don't even remember exactly why anymore, maybe because I thought I looked stupid, or maybe because I was overthinking something that didn't matter. But I remember you didn't care; you just laughed, you were just there, and somehow that small moment stayed with me. I think that's something I've always loved about memories: the things that stay with us are not always the moments we expect. Sometimes it's not the big events, but a waffle, a laugh, a random afternoon, or a moment where someone made you feel comfortable enough to stop worrying for a second. I think about how much time we spend being ashamed of ourselves, ashamed of the things we did, the things we didn't do, or the person we think we are. And sometimes shame becomes so loud that it starts rewriting the entire story, convincing us that one mistake is all people will ever see or that one moment defines everything. But I don't think that's true; people are much bigger than the moments they regret. I think about this trick I always come back to: when I feel overwhelmed by shame or regret, I think about a line I love, or a poem that somehow found me at the exact right moment, and I remind myself that it would not have reached me if everything in my life had happened differently. Not that way, not with those exact words, and not with that exact feeling. It doesn't mean everything happens for a reason or that pain is something we should be grateful for, but it means that our experiences become part of us. They shape the way we see things, the way we understand other people, and the way we love. I think that's what you have always done: you take things that happened to you and somehow turn them into something you can understand. You search for meaning, and you search for answers, sometimes maybe too much, but I think that comes from wanting to understand yourself. I remember this feeling of constantly trying to communicate something that feels impossible to explain, trying to put words to something that only exists as a feeling, or trying to describe something you only know in your bones. I think everyone has something like that, something they carry that language never fully reaches. Maybe that's why we use songs, books, pictures, and memories, because sometimes words alone are not enough; sometimes you need something else to hold the feeling. I think about the places we are told not to return to: the corners of our lives where we were once happy. People always say to move forward and never look back, but I don't think it's always that simple. Sometimes looking back hurts because it reminds you of what you lost, or because it reminds you of who you used to be. But I don't think remembering is always the enemy; sometimes remembering is how we understand, and sometimes the past is not something we need to run away from, but something we need to learn how to carry. I know you are afraid of being misunderstood; that's one of the things I notice about you. You spend so much time trying to explain yourself, not because you don't know what you feel (I think you know exactly what you feel), but maybe you just worry that when you finally say it, the words won't be enough. That someone will hear the sentence but miss the entire meaning behind it. And I understand that fear, because some things are too big to fit into a conversation, take years to explain, or are just felt. But Jayme, I hope you know that you don't always have to find the perfect words for someone to understand you. Sometimes the right person notices before you even finish explaining: they see the hesitation, the silence, and the things you don't say. I think that's what makes moments like Yellow River matter, because you didn't need me to explain why I was embarrassed, and you didn't need to ask a hundred questions. You just made it okay. And maybe that's one of the things I'll always remember: not that you fixed anything or made every insecurity disappear, but just that for a moment, I didn't feel like I had to hide. I could just be there, eating a Nutella waffle, being embarrassed, and laughing with you. Maybe that's what I mean when I say I miss the little things, not the perfect moments, but just the ones where I felt like myself, the ones where I felt seen.
